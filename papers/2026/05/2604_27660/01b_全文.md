However, many real-world tasks require LMs to learn from complex contexts and leverage new knowledge rather than parametric knowledge to reason and solve them effectively [ 26 , 36 , 47 ] . Previous works refer to this capability as context learning [ 12 ] . Effective context learning enables models to reason beyond their pre-trained knowledge and solve complex, domain-specific tasks by learning directly from rich contextual information, much as humans do. For example, it allows LMs to rapidly make use of previously unseen product documentation to generate step-by-step operational procedures or troubleshoot issues. Despite the central role of context learning in solving real-world tasks, it has been largely overlooked in current research [ 16 ] . A key obstacle is the diversity of real-world contexts: contextual knowledge can be conveyed through books, experimental data, and search results, among others, making context learning fundamentally difficult. An intuitive paradigm is inference-time skill augmentation [ 3 ] , i.e., extracting rules and procedures from context into natural-language skills that encode reusable procedural knowledge for LMs. This approach has proven effective in many agent tasks [ 42 , 49 , 37 ] , e.g., coding agents [ 24 ] . However, this paradigm faces two fundamental challenges in context learning scenarios. Specifically, (1) Prohibitive Cost for Manual Skill Annotation : Existing agent skill libraries are predominantly constructed through human annotation [ 18 , 20 , 41 ] . However, this paradigm breaks down in context learning scenarios, where contexts are long, technically dense, and domain-specific. Curating high-quality skills requires annotators to fully internalize complex, multi-section documents, a process that is both cognitively demanding and economically infeasible, rendering human-curated skill construction impractical at scale. (2) Lack of External Feedback for Automated Skill Construction : Unlike verifiable tasks such as coding or mathematical reasoning [ 7 , 31 ] , where skill quality can be assessed via execution feedback or ground-truth comparison, context learning tasks lack automatic feedback to evaluate whether extracted skills faithfully and completely capture context-specific knowledge. Given only the context, there is no external feedback signal to tell whether a generated skill is useful or whether critical knowledge has been omitted. This feedback-free nature renders automated skill construction pipelines inapplicable [ 1 , 44 , 46 ] . Together, these challenges highlight the need for an automated framework that discovers skills from the context without relying on either human annotation or external feedback signals. In this paper, we propose Ctx2Skill shown in Figure 1 , which is designed to autonomously discover, refine, and select skills directly from complex contexts via skill-optimized self-play, requiring neither human annotation nor external feedback. At the core of Ctx2Skill is a multi-agent self-play loop [ 10 , 22 , 48 , 21 ] comprising two competing but co-evolving roles: a Challenger agent, which generates a batch of tasks and associated rubrics [ 15 ] based on context and its skill set, aiming to probe deep contextual understanding, and a Reasoner agent, which reads the context and attempts to solve these tasks guided by its current skill set. The core idea of Ctx2Skill is that the two agents co-evolve by iteratively updating their respective skill sets rather than model parameters. Specifically, a neutral Judge agent evaluates the Reasoner ’s responses against each rubric, producing feedback on whether it passes. Crucially, both the Challenger and Reasoner evolve through accumulated natural-language skills: failed cases are routed to dedicated Proposer and Generator agents on the Reasoner side to diagnose missing contextual knowledge and update skills accordingly, while easily solved cases are routed to the Challenger side to strengthen its task and rubric generation strategies, ensuring sustained adversarial pressure. The updated skills are carried forward into the next iteration, allowing both sides to continuously co-evolve through failure-driven textual feedback without any parameter updates. Furthermore, we identify a key risk in this multi-agent self-play loop, namely adversarial collapse phenomenon: as iterations progress, the Challenger may generate increasingly extreme tasks while the Reasoner ’s skills over-specialize to these pathological cases, resulting in redundant skill accumulation that degrades generalization. To address this, we further propose a Cross-time Replay mechanism that selects the skill set that best balances performance across representative cases, ensuring robust and generalizable skill evolution. At inference time, the resulting context-specific Reasoner skills can be plugged into any LM to enhance its context learning capability on arbitrary tasks over the same context, including those unseen during the self-play loop. We evaluate our Ctx2Skill on four context learning tasks from CL-Bench [ 12 ] , including Domain Knowledge Reasoning, Rule System Application, Procedural Task Execution, and Empirical Discovery &amp; Simulation. Ctx2Skill consistently improves solving rate across all four categories and multiple backbone models: it raises GPT-4.1 [ 27 ] from 11.1 % 11.1\% to 16.5 % 16.5\% , GPT-5.1 [ 28 ] from 21.1 % 21.1\% to 25.8 % 25.8\% , and GPT-5.2 [ 28 ] from 18.2 % 18.2\% to 21.4 % 21.4\% , demonstrating that autonomously discovered context-specific skills provide substantial and generalizable gains. 2 Related Work Context Learning. Current language models excel at tasks whose relevant knowledge was present during pre-training [ 9 , 28 , 33 , 34 ] . However, many real-world tasks require models to learn from complex contexts and leverage new knowledge to reason effectively, a capability termed context learning [ 12 ] . This context learning capability is essential in practice: a physician consults newly released clinical guidelines to adjust treatment, an engineer reads documentation to execute procedures, and a researcher induces patterns from experimental data to form hypotheses. However, prior work finds that LMs have yet to master this capability [ 12 ] . The core difficulty is that contextual knowledge spans diverse formats, from books to experimental datasets, and the relevant rules and procedures are often embedded implicitly, requiring deep comprehension rather than surface-level retrieval. Ctx2Skill directly targets this challenge by autonomously discovering reusable, context-specific skills from the contexts, equipping any LM to better learn from and reason over complex contexts. Skills for LMs. Skills are natural-language modules that encode reusable procedural knowledge to augment LMs at inference time [ 2 , 3 ] , and skill augmentation has been widely validated across agent tasks [ 32 ] , e.g., coding [ 24 ] and web navigation [ 42 ] . Early skill libraries are predominantly constructed through human annotation [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 41 ] , which is effective but does not scale, especially for context learning scenarios where contexts are long, technically dense, and domain-specific. Recent work has shifted toward automated skill construction. AutoSkill [ 44 ] extracts reusable behaviors from interaction traces as lifelong versioned artifacts, and AutoRefine [ 30 ] converts agent trajectories into reusable expertise. CoEvoSkills [ 45 ] generates multi-file skill packages and refines them through generator–verifier co-evolution, while EvoSkill [ 1 ] performs failure-driven refinement into structured skill folders. SkillX [ 40 ] distills the agent trajectories into a hierarchical skill knowledge base and iteratively refines them through execution feedback. However, these methods rely on external feedback signals [ 25 ] , such as execution feedback, ground-truth comparison, or task-completion rewards, to evaluate and improve skill quality, which are not available in context learning scenarios where there is no automatic feedback. A separate line of work tries to internalize skills into model parameters. SKILL0 [ 23 ] uses in-context reinforcement learning (RL) to absorb skills, and SkillRL [ 43 ] builds a hierarchical skill bank via RL-guided distillation from teacher trajectories. These approaches require parameter access, making them inapplicable to closed-source models, and sacrificing the interpretability of natural-language skill documents. In this paper, we propose Ctx2Skill , a self-evolving framework designed to directly discover and evolve skills from complex contexts alone, which requires no human annotation, no external feedback, and no parameter updates. 3 Methodology 3.1 Overview Context learning tasks [ 12 ] require LMs to leverage contextual knowledge from long, technically dense contexts to reason and solve tasks effectively. An intuitive paradigm for context learning tasks is inference-time skill augmentation, i.e., extracting the corresponding rules and procedures from context into natural-language skills . Turning this idea into a working framework, however, faces two difficulties: (1) prohibitive cost for manual skill annotation and (2) lack of external feedback for automated skill construction. In this paper, we introduce Ctx2Skill shown in Figure 2 , a self-evolving framework that autonomously discovers, refines, and selects context-specific skills without human supervision or external feedback. At its core, a skill-optimized self-play loop (§ 3.3 ) co-evolves two competing sides: a Challenger probes the context with tasks and rubrics guided by its own skill set, a Reasoner solves them under its current skill set, and a neutral Judge returns binary verdicts that route each outcome to a side-specific Proposer – Generator pair, which diagnoses the exposed weakness and edits that side’s skills accordingly, so that both sides co-evolve without human annotation or external feedback. Furthermore, a key risk in this self-play framework is adversarial collapse : the Challenger may generate increasingly extreme tasks while the Reasoner ’s skills over-specialize to these pathological cases, resulting in redundant skill accumulation that degrades generalization. To address this, we further propose a Cross-Time Replay mechanism (§ 3.4 ) that selects the skill set that best balances performance across representative cases, ensuring robust and generalizable skill evolution. In this way, Ctx2Skill autonomously constructs context-specific skills that can be plugged into any LM at inference time to enhance context learning capability without parameter updates. 3.2 Problem Formulation A context learning task [ 12 ] consists of a given context C C whose content may lie outside the LM’s pretraining corpus, a set of tasks 𝒯 = { t j } \mathcal{T}=\{t_{j}\} whose correct answers depend on C C , and a set of binary rubrics ℛ j = { r j , k } \mathcal{R}_{j}=\{r_{j,k}\} . Given an answer a j a_{j} produced by an LM π \pi , the task is considered solved only when every rubric passes, we define a solving indicator y j ​ ( π , C ) y_{j}(\pi;C) for task t j t_{j} as: y j ( π ; C ) = ∏ k 𝕀 [ r j , k ( a j ) = pass ] , a j ∼ π ( ⋅ ∣ C , t j ) . y_{j}(\pi;C)=\prod_{k}\mathbb{I}\bigl[r_{j,k}(a_{j})=\mathrm{pass}\bigr],\qquad a_{j}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid C,t_{j}). (1) Our goal is to enable the LM π \pi to solve tasks over a previously unseen context C C without any parameter update or external feedback. To this end, we introduce a natural-language skill set 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , a short Markdown file pre-pended to the system prompt of the LM at inference time: a j ∼ π ( ⋅ ∣ 𝒮 , C , t j ) . a_{j}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\mathcal{S},C,t_{j}). (2) During the skills construction of our proposed Ctx2Skill , 𝒮 \mathcal{S} is instantiated as two separate skill sets: 𝒮 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}} for the Reasoner agent and 𝒮 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}} for the Challenger agent. At inference time, only the Reasoner ’s skill set 𝒮 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}} is deployed with an LM for context-specific tasks evaluation. Figure 2: The overview of Ctx2Skill . (b) Cross-Time Replay Mechanism re-evaluates historical Reasoner skill candidates on representative tasks, selecting the most balanced skill set for unseen downstream tasks. 3.3 Ctx2Skill Framework Building a skill set for an unseen context C C faces two obstacles: human annotation is costly to read C C and write down its contextual knowledge, and no external feedback signal can tell us whether a proposed skill is faithful and useful to C C . To address both, Ctx2Skill runs a multi-agent self-play loop that autonomously discovers and refines context-specific skills from context C C alone. The core idea of Ctx2Skill is to co-evolve two skill sets, skill set 𝒮 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}} for the Reasoner and skill set 𝒮 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}} for the Challenger , through N N iterations of failure-driven textual edits, so that the Reasoner gradually accumulates the contextual knowledge of the context C C while the Challenger keeps probing aspects not yet mastered. Five frozen-LM agent roles carry out this self-play loop. At iteration i i , given the context C C and skill set 𝒮 i − 1 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i-1} , the Challenger produces a batch of tasks with corresponding rubrics; the Reasoner , guided by context C C and its skills 𝒮 i − 1 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i-1} , attempts each task; the neutral Judge returns per-task binary verdicts and partitions the batch into failed and solved tasks. Each group is then routed to its corresponding side through a Proposer – Generator pair: failed cases flow to the Reasoner side, where the Proposer diagnoses which contextual knowledge is missing and the Generator updates 𝒮 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}} accordingly; solved cases flow to the Challenger side, where the corresponding pair tightens 𝒮 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}} so that the next iteration sustains adversarial pressure. The updated 𝒮 i R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i} and 𝒮 i C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i} are carried into iteration i + 1 i{+}1 , and no prompt is ever conditioned on the opposing side’s skill set, keeping the two sides strictly adversarial. We now define the five agent roles as follows at the iteration i i : • Challenger : Given the context C C and its current skill set 𝒮 i − 1 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i-1} , the Challenger produces a batch of M M tasks { t m } m = 1 M \{t_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M} , each equipped with corresponding rubrics ℛ m \mathcal{R}_{m} . The rubrics are designed so that a correct answer requires inducing the rules of C C rather than paraphrasing surface spans. By conditioning on its skills 𝒮 i − 1 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i-1} , the Challenger adapts its probing strategy across iterations, sustaining adversarial pressure as the Reasoner improves. • Reasoner : Given C C , a task t m t_{m} , and its current skill set 𝒮 i − 1 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i-1} , the Reasoner produces an answer a m a_{m} . While the raw context C C contains all necessary knowledge, it is often long and technically dense; the skill set 𝒮 i − 1 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i-1} distills the most relevant rules and procedures from C C into a concise, structured form, allowing the Reasoner to directly apply contextual knowledge that would otherwise need to be extracted from scratch at every task. • Judge : Given a task and rubrics generated from the Challenger , and the generated answer from the Reasoner , the Judge returns a per-rubric binary verdict z m , k = 𝕀 [ r m , k ( a m ) = pass ] z_{m,k}=\mathbb{I}[r_{m,k}(a_{m})=\mathrm{pass}] and a solving indicator y m = ∏ k z m , k y_{m}=\prod_{k}z_{m,k} . The Judge is a strict evaluator: the task is considered solved if and only if the answer satisfies all associated rubrics. • Proposer (one per side) : The Judge ’s binary verdicts identify which tasks failed or solved, but not why . The Proposer therefore collects all routed cases of its side, where each case comprises the task and rubrics from the Challenger , and the Reasoner ’s answer, and jointly analyzes them against the current skill set. Rather than diagnosing each case in isolation, the Proposer synthesizes common failure or success patterns across cases into a high-level diagnosis specifying an action (add or merge), the target skill name, a description, and a justification. The Reasoner Proposer is designed to take the failed cases ℱ i \mathcal{F}_{i} together with 𝒮 i − 1 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i-1} , and diagnose which contextual knowledge is missing or misrepresented by examining both the incorrect answers and the rubrics they violated. On the other hand, the Challenger Proposer takes the easily solved cases 𝒫 i \mathcal{P}_{i} together with 𝒮 i − 1 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i-1} , and identifies gaps in the current task and rubric generation strategy by inspecting how the Reasoner solved them with its existing skills. • Generator (one per side) : The Proposer ’s output is a high-level diagnosis describing what should change and why, but not the concrete skill content itself. The Generator materializes this diagnosis into an actual skill set. Given the diagnosis and the current skill set, it returns a complete replacement skill set that adds or merges entries while preserving every unrelated entry. The Reasoner Generator incorporates the diagnosed missing contextual knowledge into 𝒮 i − 1 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i-1} to produce the new skill set 𝒮 i R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i} . The Challenger Generator tightens 𝒮 i − 1 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i-1} so that the next iteration probes the Reasoner ’s remaining weaknesses, producing skills 𝒮 i C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i} . To summarize, at iteration i i , the Challenger first produces a batch of tasks and rubrics { ( t m , ℛ m ) } m = 1 M \{(t_{m},\mathcal{R}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M} based on the context C C ; the Reasoner attempts to answer each t m t_{m} ; the Judge evaluates every rubric and partitions the batch into failed cases ℱ i = { m : y m = 0 } \mathcal{F}_{i}=\{m:y_{m}=0\} and solved cases 𝒫 i = { m : y m = 1 } \mathcal{P}_{i}=\{m:y_{m}=1\} based on the solving indicator y m y_{m} . Each group is then routed to its corresponding side: • The Reasoner Proposer jointly analyzes all failed cases ℱ i \mathcal{F}_{i} against 𝒮 i − 1 R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i-1} and synthesizes a high-level diagnosis; the Reasoner Generator then materializes it into 𝒮 i R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i} . • The Challenger Proposer jointly analyzes all solved cases 𝒫 i \mathcal{P}_{i} against 𝒮 i − 1 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i-1} and synthesizes a high-level diagnosis; the Challenger Generator then materializes it into 𝒮 i C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i} . The updated skill sets 𝒮 i R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i} and 𝒮 i C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_{i} are carried forward into iteration i + 1 i{+}1 . No prompt is ever conditioned on the opposing side’s skill set, keeping the two sides strictly adversarial. 3.4 Cross-Time Replay Mechanism The framework of Ctx2Skill detailed in § 3.3 intentionally strengthens the Challenger across iterations through updates to 𝒮 C \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}} , sustaining adversarial pressure as the Reasoner improves. However, this design introduces an inherent tension that we term adversarial collapse . As iterations progress, the Challenger generates increasingly extreme tasks that concentrate on the Reasoner ’s residual weaknesses, gradually deviating from the representative knowledge of the context C C . Since the Reasoner ’s skill updates are failure-driven, its skills over-specialize to these pathological cases, resulting in redundant skill accumulation that degrades generalization. Moreover, this collapse is undetectable within the loop: each iteration’s Judge only evaluates the Challenger ’s newly generated tasks, providing no signal on whether contextual knowledge mastered in earlier iterations has been corrupted by subsequent edits. Returning the last iteration Reasoner ’s skills 𝒮 N R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{N} unconditionally is therefore unreliable; we instead select the most generalizable skill set among candidates { 𝒮 1 R , … , 𝒮 N R } \{\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{N}\} . To address this, we propose a cross-time replay mechanism that selects the most generalizable skill set from the candidates { 𝒮 1 R , … , 𝒮 N R } \{\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{N}\} using representative probes collected during self-play itself. Two small probe sets are curated incrementally without any external supervision: at each iteration i i , the failed task with the lowest rubric pass rate is added to the hard probe set 𝒬 h \mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{h}} , and the solved task with the fewest rubrics is added to the easy probe set 𝒬 e \mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{e}} , capturing the hardest failure and the easiest success observed at each iteration. Once the self-play loop terminates, the Reasoner π R \pi^{\mathrm{R}} re-answers every task in both probe sets under each candidate skill set 𝒮 i R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i} , and the Judge re-evaluates every rubric, yielding the solving indicator y q y_{q} (i.e., whether all rubrics pass) for each probe task q q . We define the Laplace-smoothed [ 13 ] solving rates ρ h ​ ( i ) \rho^{\mathrm{h}}(i) and ρ e ​ ( i ) \rho^{\mathrm{e}}(i) as the proportion of tasks solved in the hard and easy probe sets under the context C C and skills 𝒮 i R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i} , respectively: ρ h ​ ( i ) = ∑ q ∈ 𝒬 h y q ​ ( π R , C , 𝒮 i R ) + 1 | 𝒬 h | + 1 , ρ e ​ ( i ) = ∑ q ∈ 𝒬 e y q ​ ( π R , C , 𝒮 i R ) + 1 | 𝒬 e | + 1 . \rho^{h}(i)=\frac{\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{h}}}y_{q}(\pi^{\mathrm{R}};C,\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i})+1}{|\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{h}}|+1},\qquad\rho^{e}(i)=\frac{\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{e}}}y_{q}(\pi^{\mathrm{R}};C,\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i})+1}{|\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{e}}|+1}. (3) The selected skill set is the one that maximizes the product of these two solving rates: 𝒮 ⋆ R = 𝒮 i ⋆ R , i ⋆ = arg ⁡ max i ⁡ ( ρ h ​ ( i ) ⋅ ρ e ​ ( i ) ) . \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\star}=\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i^{\star}},\qquad i^{\star}=\arg\max_{i}\left(\rho^{\mathrm{h}}(i)\cdot\rho^{\mathrm{e}}(i)\right). (4) The multiplicative form is essential: a skill set that resolves late, idiosyncratic failures at the cost of regressing on easier tasks incurs a penalty on ρ e ​ ( i ) \rho^{\mathrm{e}}(i) and is rejected; conversely, one that solves every easy probe but fails every hard one is rejected through ρ h ​ ( i ) \rho^{\mathrm{h}}(i) . Meanwhile, Laplace smoothing keeps the product well-defined when a probe set is empty for a given iteration At inference time, the final skill set 𝒮 ⋆ R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\star} is prepended to the Reasoner ’s system prompt for any unseen task t u t_{u} over context C C , and the Reasoner answers conditioned on ( 𝒮 ⋆ R , C , t u ) (\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\star},C,t_{u}) . Since 𝒮 ⋆ R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\star} encodes reusable contextual knowledge of C C rather than task-specific solutions, it generalizes to arbitrary tasks over the same context. Moreover, 𝒮 ⋆ R \mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\star} is produced once per context and reused across all | 𝒯 | |\mathcal{T}| unseen tasks, so the Ctx2Skill cost amortizes over | 𝒯 | |\mathcal{T}| with no additional cost per deployed query. 4 Experiments In this section, we conduct experiments and analyses to show the advantages of our Ctx2Skill . 4.1 Settings Evaluation. We ablate each component of Ctx2Skill on both the GPT-4.1 and GPT-5.1 (Table 3 , Ablation Study block). Removing Challenger skills evolving causes the largest drop, confirming that sustained adversarial pressure is essential for the Reasoner to discover contextual knowledge progressively. The Cross-Time Replay mechanism is the second most impactful: without it, the last-iteration skills suffer from adversarial collapse. Within Cross-Time Replay , the hard probe set contributes more than the easy probe set, and removing Laplace smoothing also degrades performance. Merging the Proposer and Generator into a single agent yields a modest but consistent decline, supporting the decoupling of diagnosis from skill materialization. We also discuss potential concerns about Ctx2Skill , e.g., reward hacking, in Appendix D . 5 Conclusion We presented Ctx2Skill , a self-evolving framework that autonomously discovers, refines, and selects context-specific skills from complex contexts without human annotation or external feedback. Through a skill-optimized self-play loop, a Challenger and a Reasoner co-evolve their skill sets via failure-driven textual edits, while a Cross-Time Replay mechanism prevents adversarial collapse by selecting the most generalizable skill set across iterations. Experiments on CL-bench demonstrate that Ctx2Skill consistently and substantially improves context learning performance across multiple backbone models and task categories, and that the resulting skills are transferable across models. Appendix D Discussion We discuss three potential concerns regarding the validity of Ctx2Skill ’s improvements: (1) whether the gains stem from reward hacking , i.e., the skills exploiting the preferences of the specific Judge model rather than encoding genuine contextual knowledge; (2) whether the Challenger -generated tasks overlap with the CL-bench test tasks, causing test set contamination ; and (3) whether the discovered skills are truly context-specific rather than generic answering strategies. Our analyses show that none of these concerns holds, confirming that the improvements of Ctx2Skill come from genuinely learned, context-specific knowledge. Analysis of Potential Reward Hacking. In Ctx2Skill , the Judge that provides binary feedback during the self-play loop is GPT-5.1, which is also the LLM-as-a-judge verifier of the CL-bench evaluation protocol [ 12 ] . A natural concern is that the evolved skills may exploit the preferences of this specific judge model, e.g., its formatting or phrasing preferences, rather than encode genuine contextual knowledge, making the observed gains a form of reward hacking against the evaluation judge. To examine this, we decouple the two judges: on the GPT-5.2-based methods, we replace the Judge in the self-play loop with GPT-5.2 while keeping GPT-5.1 as the evaluation judge, and rerun Ctx2Skill on all 500 500 contexts. As shown in Table 11 , the resulting skills achieve a 21.2 % 21.2\% overall solving rate, nearly identical to the 21.4 % 21.4\% obtained with the default GPT-5.1 loop judge, with all per-category differences within 0.4 % 0.4\% . The gains of Ctx2Skill therefore do not depend on the loop judge matching the evaluation judge, indicating that the discovered skills encode judge-agnostic contextual knowledge rather than exploiting the preferences of a specific judge model.
